1 · The Fabry–Pérot interferometer and the optical cavity

      
A Fabry–Pérot interferometer (FPI) is two parallel, partially reflecting surfaces
      separated by a distance d; coated surfaces form an FP etalon (introduced by Charles
      Fabry and Alfred Pérot in 1899 as a spectroscopy tool). Light entering the gap is reflected
      back and forth many times, and a small fraction leaves at every interaction: these many
      delayed copies of the incident wave interfere with each other.

      
An optical cavity (resonator) is the more general idea: reflecting elements that
      confine light so it reproduces itself after one round trip. The two-mirror FP cavity is the
      simplest standing-wave resonator; flat–flat gives the classical etalon, curved mirrors give
      the spherical-mirror resonator used in most lasers.

      

        
      

      
The same structure plays two roles: passive — an external light is analysed
      or filtered (spectrometer, narrow-band filter, frequency reference); and active —
      a gain medium is inserted and the mirrors provide feedback that turns an amplifier into an
      oscillator, selecting which frequencies and spatial patterns can oscillate (Schawlow–Townes 1958;
      Maiman’s 1960 ruby laser used parallel coated crystal faces as its FP cavity).

2.6 Resolving power and tuning

      
The resonance can be tuned by changing d (piezo-scanning), θ (tilting) or n (temperature,
      gas pressure). Sweeping d while a photodiode records the transmitted power flattens the
      incident spectrum into peaks repeated every FSR; with a lens, the angular dependence produces
      the familiar concentric ring pattern, with the highest orders near the centre.

    


    

      
3 · The FP cavity as a laser resonator

      
A laser needs a gain medium, a pump, and optical feedback. A single pass amplifies intensity
      by only egℓ; between two mirrors the light traverses the medium many times, so weak
      gain can build a strong field. The cavity also selects the frequencies and transverse patterns
      that oscillate, giving the laser its narrow linewidth and defined beam.

4 · Longitudinal modes and transverse modes

      
A mode is a field distribution that reproduces itself after one round trip. It is
      labelled TEMmnq: index q describes the variation along the axis, m, n across the
      beam.


      
4.1 Longitudinal modes

      
Setting the round-trip phase to a multiple of 2π gives standing waves with an integer
      number of half-wavelengths between the mirrors:

      

      
(In a dispersive medium use the group index ng in the spacing.) The spacing is
      exactly the FSR of the equivalent FP interferometer. The gain medium amplifies a finite band
      Δνg, so the laser oscillates on the modes inside the band whose gain exceeds
      threshold; approximately Nq ≈ Δνg,above-threshold/ΔνFSR
      modes. Shortening the cavity raises the spacing — when the FSR exceeds the gain bandwidth
      only one longitudinal mode survives.

5 · Cavity configuration and stability


      
5.1 Ray-matrix derivation

      
Not every pair of mirrors traps light. One round trip is the product of free-space (L) and
      reflection-mirror (f = R_c/2) ABCD matrices:

      

      
Since det M = 1, rays stay bounded only if |A + D|/2 ≤ 1. With the cavity g-parameters

      

      
one finds (A + D)/2 = 2g₁g₂ − 1, hence the stability condition

      

      
The same condition emerges from Gaussian optics: a beam fits the cavity only when its
      wavefronts coincide with the mirrors, R(zi) = Rci, which has a real
      solution only for the same range of g₁g₂ (Step 6).


      
5.2 The stability diagram

      
The stable region lies between the axes and the hyperbola g₁g₂ = 1, in the first and third
      quadrants. Boundary cavities (plane–plane, hemispherical, confocal, concentric) are
      marginally stable: a tiny spacing or tilt change decides whether they work.

5.4 How the cavity position shapes laser behaviour

      
    
        
  • Mode size & gain extraction. Large fundamental modes (near
        plane–plane or concentric) fill more gain volume and extract more power but invite several
        transverse modes; small modes (near-confocal) concentrate intensity and lower threshold but
        sample less of the medium. End-pumped solid-state designs mode-match the pump spot to the
        cavity waist.
    • 
        
  • Misalignment sensitivity. Near the boundaries the mode size diverges or
        collapses; small tilts move the mode axis far, and output drops quickly. Mid-stable designs
        (g₁g₂ ≈ 0.3–0.7) tolerate tilt and thermal drift — the commercial default.
    • 
        
  • Transverse mode content / M². Large modes have closely spaced
        transverse frequencies and little loss discrimination → multimode oscillation degrades M².
        A limiting aperture (small Fresnel number) is an effective mode filter.
    • 
        
  • Output divergence. θ_div = λ/(πw₀): large-waist (long-Rayleigh) cavities
        give well-collimated output.
    • 
        
  • Thermal lens. Pump heating changes the effective g-parameters; a cavity
        near a stability edge can flip unstable with pump power. Designs must stay stable across the
        thermal-lens range.
    • 
        
  • Unstable resonators. In high-gain lasers, unstable cavities (g₁g₂<0
        or >1) let the beam expand to fill the gain volume, output coupled around the mirror edge.
    • 
      

    


    

      
6 · Practical example and applications


      
6.1 Worked example: a helium–neon laser

      
He–Ne at λ = 632.8 nm, L = 0.30 m, flat high reflector + concave output coupler
      Rc2 = 0.50 m: g₁ = 1, g₂ = 0.4, g₁g₂ = 0.40 — comfortably stable. The beam follows
      from Sec. 5.3; the neon gain is Doppler-broadened to ≈ 1.5 GHz so about three longitudinal
      modes fit; with the narrow discharge-tube aperture suppressing higher orders, the laser runs
      TEM₀₀. If Rc2 were 0.30 m (hemispherical limit, g₂ = 0) the cavity would sit on the
      stability boundary and misalignment sensitivity would explode.

6.2 Semiconductor laser diode

      
An edge-emitting diode is an FP cavity whose mirrors are the cleaved crystal facets. With
      n ≈ 3.5, R = ((n−1)/(n+1))² ≈ 0.31 — enough because the gain is large. For L = 300 µm the
      mirror-loss term is ln(1/R)/L ≈ 39 cm⁻¹, and with ng ≈ 4 the mode spacing is
      c/(2ngL) ≈ 125 GHz ≈ 0.3 nm at 850 nm: a multimode FP diode emits a comb of such
      lines under its gain bandwidth. (Transverse modes are set by the semiconductor waveguide —
      the stability analysis above does not apply.)

6.3 The confocal scanning interferometer

      
A spherical-mirror FP with Rc1 = Rc2 = L (confocal) is the standard
      instrument for observing laser modes. A ray retraces its path after two round trips,
      so the effective FSR is c/4L, and all TEMlm orders are transmitted at nearly the
      same frequencies (uniform c/4L comb) — an instrument insensitive to alignment and to the beam’s
      transverse profile, unlike the plane–plane etalon. A piezo scans L; the photodiode traces the
      tested laser’s mode comb (e.g. the three He–Ne modes above).

6.4 Other applications

      
    
        
  • Filters & telecom. Thin etalons as narrow-band filters in WDM and as
        frequency references for locking.
    • 
        
  • Frequency stabilisation. High-finesse cavity as frequency discriminator;
        Pound–Drever–Hall locking narrows the laser linewidth far below the cavity linewidth.
    • 
        
  • Gravitational-wave detection. LIGO’s kilometre arms are FP cavities with
        finesse of several hundred, multiplying the effective path length.
    • 
        
  • Cavity-enhanced sensing. Long effective path in high-finesse cavities
        makes weak absorption measurable; resonances shift with length, index, temperature, pressure.
    • 
      

    


    

      
7 · Conclusion

      
The FP structure combines multiple-beam interference and optical feedback. As an
      interferometer, its transmission is the Airy function with FSR c/2nd and finesse
      π√R/(1−R) defining the resolving power. As a laser resonator, the same geometry fixes the
      longitudinal spacing c/2nL and, through the mirror curvatures, the Hermite–Gaussian transverse
      modes. Stability requires 0 ≤ g₁g₂ ≤ 1, and the position inside that region sets mode size,
      mode content, alignment tolerance and thermal robustness — practical designs avoid the
      boundaries unless a specific property (large mode volume or tiny waist) is wanted.
